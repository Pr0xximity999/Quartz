# Part 1 - Lesson (part 1)
## What is a system?
Systems are two or more parts that form a whole. Everything around you is a system. 
A system is something interconnected which makes the whole better/more effective than the sum of its parts. Almost anything that involves a human, by definition, is a system.

When a part of a system breaks or gets re-arranged, it might influence the system.
A group of things that do not interconnect or interact is called a heap/collection.

### Feedback loops
Take a wolf and a rabbit (or a group of). If you think “wolf eats rabbit”, you think one way. 
If you think “wolf eating rabbit leads to less rabbit, which leads to less wolf, which leads to more rabbit, which leads to more wolf, which leads to less rabbit….”, you think in feedback loops.

#### Negative feedback loop
This one is actually the good one, surprisingly. A negative feedback loop reduces the effect of change and helps maintain balance. Negative feedback loops have balance.

#### Positive feedback loop
Positive feedback loops cause an spiraling-out-of-control effect, like global warming. It increases the effect of the change and produces instability. Parts of the loop influence each other in an increasing way.


## Systems thinking
Understanding a system by examining the links and interactions between elements that make up the system, is systems thinking. Thinking in a way that keeps in mind how things are connected and change one another, can make the creation of a system more robust or less prone to failure, opposed to linear thinking (affect and cause)

### Basic elements of a system
Some things that may be in a system when creating a solution might be:
- Stakeholders
- Objects and technologies
- Relationships
- Feedback loops
- Environmental factors

Keep in mind what the bounds of your system is. Think to yourself “does this influence my system or the system outside of mine?” before adding something to your system.

## A habit of mind
WE use systems thinking as a framework to help develop habits of mind that empower us to creatively examine complex problems. Sustainability problems are by definition complex problems.

Habits of a systems thinker:
- Seeking to understand the big picture
- Seeing patterns/trends in systems
- Recognizing how a system’s structure causes its behavior
- Identifying cause and effect relationships
- Surfacing and testing assumptions
- Finding where unintended consequences might arise
- Finding leverage points to change a system
- Resisting drawing quick conclusions

**Explain a feedback loop**

**Why do we want to practice systems thinking?**
Practicing things makes them easier to do. Practicing systems thinking will make it more of a second nature and easier to do.

# Part 2 - Story telling lecture
Looking around, most people know what needs to change to improve our world, sustainability wise. But even though this is true, we don’t see it happening all around us. 

## Make a change
Changing a system is not as easy as improving one thing. You need to keep the entire system in mind, all the tiny little pieces that create the whole. This even changes between environments; there is not one solution, every solution is unique to its system.

One way to encourage being part of the new system is by giving 3 options:
- Join the system
- Create a new system
- Pay a fine (taxes, etc…)

## But keep in mind…
An ecosystem usually consists of 3 spheres of influence/relations:
- The direct ecosystem
- The value network: End users, employees, suppliers, partners, etc
- The outer sphere: Governments, labor unions, competitors, media and press, etc

All 3 of these spheres need to be kept in mind, because even the outer most sphere can make or break your solution. 
Being “technically right” isn’t enough. Sometimes you are blocked by social stigma, or a competitor is better than you. You can have great relationships with brands, but not be on the same material quality as them, cannot find a means to achieve it, and fail.

Keep in mind that not all stakeholders/variables will have as much influence. Find the key parts that will have the biggest influence and focus on those. You don’t have to always cover all bases when a base does not affect your solution.

So in conclusion, keep all the (important) variables in mind (sometimes you will find new ones during the process of creation).

## Telling a story
Stories! Conveying a message trough the telling of something. Stories are a way to let you gain a frame of reference by giving you the context trough the eyes of the teller, AKA relating to the story.

Stories give legitimacy or context to the cause. Narratives can be framed positively or negatively:
- “Sacrifice this”
- “You will lose xyz”
- “You should constrain yourself from doing this bad thing!”
or…
- “Take back control by using….”
- “You will learn/gain information by doing this”
- “Gain responsibility/agency/influence over abc with such and such”

## Positive and negative narratives
Framing a narrative in either of these will give it a different effect on the reader. One feels like a punishment when not doing them, while the other feels rewarding when you actually do them. Positive vs Negative reinforcement, hope vs fear. Nowadays most narratives are framed in a negative way because it usually puts more pressure behind the reader to actually pursue doing the thing.


## Ask yourself…
Three questions that you need to ask yourself:
1. **Who** must act?
2. What **story** are they in?
3. What makes a first step **feel safe**?

Before you can write a story about a perspective, you need to understand the perspective yourself. What are the key points? 
Research what the target audience is and what the best approach for the story can be. Make it engaging and interesting for that group. Make the first step of the story easier, a low threshold entry. Easy entry will pull in more readers to begin with. 

## Density of psyche
Not everyone is as easy to persuade. Some people are more conservative in their ways, some do not like change or are content enough with the status quo, and some just do not care enough to exert effort. This connects back to question \#1 of the previous header.

Younger people tend to be more aware and ready to change the system opposed to elderly people. People with a partner will also care more about the future than single people.
## Transparency
People like to know the direction and destination of change. No one likes uncertainty when it comes to new things, someone needs to be able to imagine themselves in the new system in a positive way. This is why relating to the target demographic is important. You cannot cater to everyone in a single story.

# Part 3 - Lesson (part 2)

## The systems iceberg
Taking actions to change a system needs some consideration too. Usually, our level of understanding is very surface level, but just like an iceberg, most of it is underneath the surface.
### Layer 1: Events (react)
These are things you can see at face value. It depicts how you react to such information.
Solutions are easy, but usually not as robust.
### Layer 2: Recognizing patterns (anticipate)
Recognizing patterns over time and returning trends will land in this category.
This layer will be used to see what you can expect from solutions.
Solutions on this layer will need constant input
### Layer 3: Underlying structures (design)
The systems and structures that cause visible patterns are in this layer
What kind of physical structures cause these patterns?
This layer depicts how structures are designed and focuses more on the lower layers.
### Layer 4: Mental models (transform)
Our thoughts and beliefs form and keep our systems in place. Changing this layer will transform how people think and will fundamentally change everything about a structure.
Solutions will go much deeper and be more robust, but also costs the most amount of effort and time. Most of all, you will need to deal with humans

## Solutionary
A solutionary is someone that creates solutions that will cause a good change in regards to humane, sustainable or just systems.
