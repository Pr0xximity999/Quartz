# The sum of two numbers
Adding two numbers together is called the **sum**.
The sum is usually mathematically denoted as a plus symbol: $+$

The two numbers used in the sum are called the **terms**.

For example: 
- The sum of 2 and 6 is: $2 + 6$
	- 2 and 6 are the terms
- The sum of 1 and 3 is: $1 + 3$
	- 1 and 3 are the terms
- The sum of 3 and 4 is: $3 + 4$
	- 3 and 4 are the terms
# The difference of two numbers
Subtracting two numbers from each other is called the **difference**.

The difference is usually mathematically denoted as a minus symbol: $-$

The two numbers used in the sum are called the **terms**.

For example: 
- The difference between 6 and 2 is: $6 - 2$
    - 6 and 2 are the terms
- The difference between 5 and 3 is: $5 - 3$
    - 5 and 3 are the terms
- The difference between 7 and 4 is: $7 - 4$
    - 7 and 4 are the terms


# The product of two numbers
Multiplying two numbers together is called the **product**.

The product is usually mathematically denoted as an x symbol: $\times$

The two numbers used in a product are called the **factors**.

The left number of the product will be added together the amount of times set by the right number (or the left number *times* the right number).

For example:
- The product of 2 and 6 is: $2 \times 6$ or $2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2$
	- 2 and 6 are the factors
- The product of 1 and 3 is: $1 \times 3$ or $1 + 1 + 1$
	- 1 and 3 are the factors
- The product of 3 and 4 is: $2 \times 6$ or $3 + 3 + 3 + 3$
	- 3 and 4 are the factors


# The quotient of two numbers
Dividing two numbers by one another is called the **quotient**.
The quotient is usually mathematically denoted as an obelus: $\div$ 

You can also write divisions as fractions, where the left number goes on the top, and the right number on the bottom

The left number is called the **dividend** (the number being divided)
- the dividend is called the numerator (top) in a fraction
The right number is called the **divisor** (the number you are dividing by)
- The divisor is called the denominator (bottom) in a fraction
Dividing means you will calculate how many times *the divisor(right) fits in the dividend(left)*.

For example:
- The quotient of 6 and 2 is: $6 \div 2$
    - 6 is the dividend, and 2 is the divisor
    - Their fraction is: $\frac{6}{2}$
- The quotient of 12 and 3 is: $12 \div 3$
    - 12 is the dividend, and 3 is the divisor
    - Their fraction is: $\frac{12}{3}$
- The quotient of 15 and 4 is: $15 \div 4$
    - 15 is the dividend, and 4 is the divisor
    - Their fraction is: $\frac{15}{4}$

# negatives inside operations
## The sum, difference, and negatives
Negative numbers can change a sum into a difference and vice versa.
Take the following equation for example:<br> $5 + -3$

$-3$ will be added to $5$. This really just means that $3$ will be **subtracted** from $5$, since adding a negative number will do the opposite.<br>So you could also write: <br> $5 - 3$

Let's take another example:<br> $-5 - -3$

$-3$ will be subtracted from $-5$. Once again, this will turn to the opposite and $3$ will be added to $-5$:<br> $-5 + 3$

This might seem confusing so let me explain.<br> A good trick to figure out whether plus or minus will turn out on top is to:
- read $+$ as **yes** 
- read $-$ as **not**.
- $++$ will be **yes yes**, which is the same as saying **yes**  ($+$)
- $+ -$ will be **yes not**, which is the same as saying **not**   ($-$)
- $-+$ will be **not yes**, which is the same as saying **not**   ($-$)
- $--$ will be **not not**, which is the same as saying **yes**   ($+$)

As you can see, the first operator will decide if its either plus or minus, the second operator will decide whether the outcome will *flip around* or not.<br>a plus($+$) will keep it the same and a minus($-$) will flip it.

>[!important]- **Keep in mind that the second operator isnt actually an operator**
>It is an indicator that the number is negative. The dual operator trick i showed is just to make adding/subtracting with negative numbers easier.

## Products and negatives
As explained at [[#The product of two numbers]], multiplication is just how many times you add a certain number together. ($5 \times 3 = 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 15$).

The same is true for negative factors: ($5 \times -3 = -3 + -3 + -3 + -3 + -3 = -15$) <br>Writing this down for two negative factors ($-5$ and $-3$) is a bit more complex, but a way to do it is like: <br> 
$$
\begin{aligned}
-5 \times 2 = -10 \\
-5 \times 1 = -5 \\
-5 \times 0 = 0 \\
-5 \times -1 = 5 \\
-5 \times -2 = 10 \\
-5 \times -3 = 15
\end{aligned}
$$
Starting with a positive numbers and looking at the pattern moving down is one way. But there's an easier way too. It is kind of like the method in [[#The sum, difference, and negatives]].

1. Take both factors in the equation: $-5$ and $-3$. 
	1. If either number is positive, write down a $+$ in front of it: $5$ -> $+5$
2. remove the numbers: $-$ and $-$ 
3. 
	- $++$ will be **yes yes**, which is the same as saying **yes**  ($+$)
	- $+ -$ will be **yes not**, which is the same as saying **not**   ($-$)
	- $-+$ will be **not yes**, which is the same as saying **not**   ($-$)
	- $--$ will be **not not**, which is the same as saying **yes**   ($+$)
4. The outcome of step 3 will decide whether the result is positive or negative: $-5 \times -3 = 15$

>[!important]- **Keep in mind that the second operator isnt actually an operator**
>It is an indicator that the number is negative. The dual operator trick i showed is just to make adding/subtracting with negative numbers easier.

>[!important]- Tip: If you have a hard time knowing the outcome at all
> Since whether the outcome is positive or negative has been found out, you can remove all minuses, calculate the outcome, and add the minuses back.<br> $-5 \times 3$ -> $5 \times 3 = 15$ -> $-5 \times 3 = -15$

>[!note]- in case the equation has more than 2 numbers
>If for example you have $5 \times -3 \times -4$, you apply the [[20 - 29 School/31 - Maths/31.04 - Order of operations|order of operations]] and do the equation in groups of 2 (in this case left to right).<br>OR, you could count the number of minuses and, if it's an even amount, the solution is positive and vice versa.

# Quotients and negatives
You can apply the rules of [[#Products and negatives|products]] for quotients as well, lets try it with $20 \div -5$:

1. Take both the dividend and divider in the equation: $20$ and $-5$. 
	1. If either number is positive, write down a $+$ in front of it: $20$ -> $+20$
2. remove the numbers: $+$ and $-$ 
3. 
	- $++$ will be **yes yes**, which is the same as saying **yes**  ($+$)
	- $+ -$ will be **yes not**, which is the same as saying **not**   ($-$)
	- $-+$ will be **not yes**, which is the same as saying **not**   ($-$)
	- $--$ will be **not not**, which is the same as saying **yes**   ($+$)
4. The outcome of step 3 will decide whether the result is positive or negative: $20 \div -5 = -4$


>[!important]- **Keep in mind that the second operator isnt actually an operator**
>It is an indicator that the number is negative. The dual operator trick i showed is just to make adding/subtracting with negative numbers easier.
>[!important] Tip: If you have a hard time knowing the outcome at all
> Since whether the outcome is positive or negative has been found out, you can remove all minuses, calculate the outcome, and add the minuses back.<br> $-20 \div 5$ -> $20 \div 5 = 4$ -> $-20 \div 5 = -20$

>[!important]- Tip: If you have a hard time knowing the outcome at all
> Since whether the outcome is positive or negative has been found out, you can remove all minuses, calculate the outcome, and add the minuses back.<br> $-5 \times 3$ -> $5 \times 3 = 15$ -> $-5 \times 3 = -15$

>[!note]- in case the equation has more than 2 numbers
>If for example you have $5 \times -3 \times -4$, you apply the [[20 - 29 School/31 - Maths/31.04 - Order of operations|order of operations]] and do the equation in groups of 2 (in this case left to right).<br>OR, you could count the number of minuses and, if it's an even amount, the solution is positive and vice versa.


## Integer part division
Usually, the quotient is defined as the greatest *whole number* of times a divisor can be subtracted from a dividend before the remainder **is below $1$**. If this is the case, the quotient of two numbers is always an integer itself, or you could say that you divide and remove the decimal numbers.

For example:
- The quotient of 15 and 4 is: $15 \div 4$
    - 15 is the dividend, and 4 is the divisor
    - Their fraction is: $\frac{15}{4}$
    - The integer part quotient would be $3$ because $15 \div 4 = 3.75$
	    - $4$ can be subtracted from $15$ **$3$** times before the remainder is below 1
